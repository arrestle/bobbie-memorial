# Letter and Email Writing

Style rules for any letter, email, or memo drafted for the user. Apply these automatically, do not wait to be asked.

## Punctuation

Never use a dash (em dash or en dash) as sentence punctuation, i.e. in place of a comma, a period, or parentheses to join or set off a clause. Rewrite that sentence as two sentences, or use a comma or parentheses instead.

Hyphens are fine, and should be kept, wherever they are part of an actual identifier or compound term: phone numbers (`704-612-0038`), case or docket numbers (`5:23-cv-00343`), hyphenated names (`Lazar-Burns`), and ordinary hyphenated words. Do not strip these.

Also never use umlauts or other diacritics. Write names without them unless the user's own source material uses them.

## Contact details

Whenever a letter or email references a specific person or firm (opposing counsel, plaintiff's counsel, a witness, an agency contact, etc.), include their full available contact details inline, not just a name. Pull whatever exists from the surrounding case files or conversation:

- Firm or organization
- Physical address
- Phone number
- Email address
- Website, if there is one

Do not make the reader look the contact up separately. The goal is that the person can be reached directly from the letter.

## Grouping by case or topic

When a letter mentions several people who each relate to a different case, matter, or topic, group them under that case/topic rather than listing people in one flat block or as walls of prose.

Use a numbered list for the cases/topics, with a bulleted sub list for each person under the case they belong to. If a case has more than one person (e.g. two attorneys on the same matter), list them as separate bullets under the same numbered case, not as separate top level items.

Each case heading should include the case name, case number, and court/district if known.

Example shape:

```
1. Case Name v. Defendant, Case No. 1:23-cv-00001, District Name
   - Attorney Name, Firm Name
     - Address: ...
     - Phone: ...
     - Email: ...
     - Note: why they're relevant
2. Other Case v. Defendant, Case No. ...
   - Attorney Name, Firm Name
     - ...
```

## Narrative flow and dates

When a run of facts tells one continuous story, one event causing or leading directly to the next, let those facts share a paragraph with flowing sentences instead of splitting every single fact into its own one-line paragraph. Reserve a new paragraph for an actual topic break, not just because another fact showed up. Weave specific dates directly into the sentence as each event happens, rather than leaving events vague or pulling all the dates out into a separate list.

Weak (choppy, dates vague):
```
I filed two internal ethics complaints. A formal Performance Improvement Plan followed the second one four days later. In February I was given 48 hours to choose between the PIP and a separation offer. I never signed it.
```

Better (flowing, dated):
```
I filed two internal ethics complaints, the second on January 31, 2026. Four days later, on February 4, Eric Hodge announced a formal Performance Improvement Plan would issue. It was delivered February 25, 2026, and I was given 48 hours to choose between the PIP and a separation offer. I never signed it.
```

This doesn't override the "short and concise" rule below, individual sentences should stay tight. This is about which related facts share a paragraph, not about writing longer paragraphs overall.

## General tone

Keep letters and emails short and concise. Prefer numbered lists of discrete asks over long paragraphs. Do not restate background the recipient already knows; get to the point.
