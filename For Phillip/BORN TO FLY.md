                                                **BORN TO FLY by Barbara Restle**

 	Walking across the tamarack of the Bloomington Airport, with the windsock indicating a gentle eastern breeze, I had agreed to fly the Cessna 150 for a flight over Lake Monroe with a passenger, a professional photographer working for a NYC publication.  The newly opened mid-west Monroe County Reservoir had made national news in 1966\.  This man’s column might not earn him a Pulitzer; however, I was enthusiastic to be able to fly him over the large watershed area. Good publicity is exactly what we need to protect this new valuable, some claim “fragile” safe water source.

I started the engine of the two-seater Cessna, taxied to the front of the airport office, and found a young camera man carrying a bulky backpack looking puzzled, ill-at-ease and definitely not ready to open his cock-pit door and hop into the co-pilot seat.  I was not about to get out of a plane with engine running, so I leaned over, opened the door and said, “You didn’t expect \--- a woman pilot, right?”

I could have been more patient but that is not who I am. I could have taken the time to assure him with ‘Yes Indeed, I have a FAA Private Pilot License,  and that today between 5% and 6%  of all pilots in the U. S. are women with global stats in a few countries higher.’

Taking note of the restricted cockpit space he opened his backpack, and selected a camera and only equipment needed for a one hour flight.  Slowly he climbed into his seat and said, “Jeez, I can’t move”. I responded with, “Wait until I fasten your shoulder harness.” This obviously was not going to be one happy passenger.

 	As we circled over the reservoir my passenger spent most of the time with a black hood over his head.  The Cessna being a high-winged plane, I flew at an angle giving him the best vantage points to aim his camera out his window. After an hour he pointed “down” and I could see he was a bit nauseous.  

As we taxied to the airport office he asked me how much he owed me?  “You do not owe me anything. This was my pleasure.  You will, however, pay at the desk for renting this plane which is not mine”.  He thanked me, informed me that my name would be added to the credits.  He hesitated and I thought he might want to apologize for the early misunderstanding. I helped him out with, “Your not alone. It happens too often”.

     There was a reason why I was willing to fly a commercial photographer over the reservoir. I was a member of the city utility board aware of a plan by an influential local business man planning  to build a factory and also his headquarters on a hill overlooking the newly opened reservoir. Members of the board voiced serious concern knowing the dangers to the lake. Tons of soil during the construction of this vast building project, even under the most carefully planned engineering, rains would dislodge soil eventually seeping into this shallow reservoir shortening its useful life.  The chairman of IU Geology Department who was the engineer of the reservoir warned us and the planning boards.  “This reservoir, with a watershed in four counties, needs to be protected.”  

Three years ago just before I was licensed I was handed over to the “Boss”. This flight instructor retired after the Korean War.  After passing my four hour FAA Pilots written exam, with me being the only woman in the room, he said there was only one flight maneuver left to pass.  At that time we were flying an aerobatic plane with canvas covered wings. He told me to ascend to 5,500 feet. Until that time all maneuvers were performed at 2,500 feet.  This was to be my final flight test. He was in the co-pilot seat.  He took control of the plane and immediately maneuvered the plane into a tight spin, spiraling downward toward the ground. The plane was now out of control, in one of the most dangerous conditions a pilot hopes to never encounter.  A moment of this, and he immediately returned the plane to straight and level flight.  I did not enjoy this level of aerobatics. He then leaned back in his seat and casually said, “Now it’s your turn.“ He did not smile. There was no question of how he accomplished this maneuver.  He did what all my training taught me never to do: he put the plane into a stall while also turning. At this level of training, gratefully I had never experienced an out-of-control plane. I did, however, remember the procedure to return to normal flight, hopefully never having to perform it. I heard him say “I’m waiting”.  I had been quickly reviewing the three procedures needed to return to level flight in my head.  The world spinning out of control can create a moment of chaos.  I lowered the nose straight down, the plane spinning and again out of control. The three recovery maneuvers were smooth.  All went well. Level flight again. I leaned back, took a deep breath and waited for the, “Back to base”.  No way.  The plane suddenly took a deep dive and was again out of control. I glanced at the instructor and he was casually looking out his window.  My second recovery went well, not as smooth but successful. I did not wait for “Let’s go home”.  I had enough. As I landed the plane on the active runway two mechanics came running towards us and with them holding each wing they helped slow us down. Unknown to me this canvas aerobatic plane had no breaks.  I stepped out of the plane and my legs turned to jelly. I grabbed the cockpit door hoping the Boss was not watching.

In the office I signed out on the daily activity sheet and handed my FAA Log Book to the Boss for him to sign his name.  I was obviously hoping that today would be my last test flight. After certifying the day’s successful maneuver, he appeared to me to be quite impatient as he said, “Barbara, why the hell don’t you sell your Aberdeen Angus Herd in Mississippi and buy a Cessna?  You should do it”.  He turned and went into his office.  Surprised?  Yes\!   

One month later I opened a letter with the returned address from the US Army Air Force requesting me to call for an appointment. There was more official ‘wordage’ that indicated this was for a pilot training program flying various military planes. The letter was burning a hole in my hand.  My children were in Bloomington North High School. The teenage years are perilous.   I whispered the words, “This is crazzy\!" Yes indeed, this was crazy for me to even think about. I reread the request again. No Way\! I then shredded the letter.

I had known that during the Korean War women pilots were not permitted in the air force but were assigned to the 78th fighter Interceptor Wing of the army. This all changed during the Vietnam War where women flew helicopters under fire to transport the wounded.  During World War II, little is known about the WAFS (Women’s Auxiliary Ferrying Squadron), a group of female commercial pilots who flew every type of aircraft in the Army Air Force.  Thirty of these women were killed but were not entitled to army survivor benefits.  

Today, about 6% of all pilots worldwide are women, and women make up roughly 5% of U.S. airline pilots, with an average age of around 44\. In countries like India, Ireland, and some parts of Africa, women account for more than 12% of airline pilots.
